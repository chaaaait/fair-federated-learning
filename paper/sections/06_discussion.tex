\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Recommended Operating Configuration}
Table~\ref{tab:recommended} solves (\ref{eq:problem}) at every budget. Without DP, FedPer is the only mechanism whose
accuracy distribution is at least as even as FedAvg's ($J=0.976$), and it adds 22.8 pts. From $\eps=8$ down to
$\eps=0.5$ local-only DP training has the largest mean gain (28.7--29.0 pts), with FedPer 1.9--2.7 pts behind it on
the mean and tied with it on the worst-10\% clients at $\eps_0$. At $\eps=0.3$ only DP-FedAvg and fine-tuning were
run, so fine-tuning (+31.0 pts) is recommended there by default. As a rule for this setting: \emph{with no or weak
privacy, use FedPer; at $\eps\ge1$, train locally or use FedPer if a shared model is needed; at $\eps\le0.5$, prefer
fine-tuning or Per-FedAvg when the worst clients matter, since they keep the highest worst-10\% accuracy}. The rule
depends on heterogeneity. In a check with a milder Dirichlet partition ($\alpha_{\mathrm{Dir}}=1.0$) at $\eps_0$, the
gains shrink to 8.6 pts for FedPer and 6.4 pts for fine-tuning, and neither keeps Jain's index at the level of
DP-FedAvg (0.974 and 0.972 versus 0.980), so (\ref{eq:problem}) recommends keeping DP-FedAvg.

\subsection{Why the Mechanisms Differ}
\emph{Label skew dominates.} With $\alpha_{\mathrm{Dir}}=0.3$ a client holds 3 to 7 classes with more than 5\% of its
data, so a model fitted to its own label mix is already a strong classifier for its own test set. This is why every
personalised mechanism gains 15.6--29.0 pts over the shared model at $\eps_0$, and why the gain falls to 6--9 pts when
the partition is milder. A single global model must spread its capacity over all ten classes, and DP noise erodes it
most on clients whose label mix differs from the average, which explains the 18.3-pt fall of the worst-10\% clients.

\emph{FedPer and local training.} FedPer shares the 140{,}064-parameter body, whose update is averaged over 20 clients,
and keeps the 1{,}290-parameter head local, so each client fits its own label mix without that head being noised by
other clients' data. That it ends 2.1 pts below purely local training at $\eps_0$ suggests the shared body brings
little useful signal under per-example DP noise at this scale; we did not test this directly, so it remains a
hypothesis. FedPer's advantage over local training is stability: it improved every client in every seed, and its
worst-10\% accuracy varied by 0.8 pts across seeds against 1.4 pts for local training.

\emph{Fine-tuning and Per-FedAvg at small budgets.} Both start from a shared model and adapt it with a few local steps.
When $\eps$ shrinks, the noise per step rises, and methods that take many noisy steps on each client (local
training, FedPer) lose more per unit $\eps$ (7.6 and 8.5 pts between $\eps=1$ and $0.5$) than fine-tuning (3.7) or
Per-FedAvg (2.0). Per-FedAvg's near-constant accuracy across budgets is consistent with a different bottleneck: each
meta-step uses two private queries, so it takes 10 updates per round instead of 20, and its accuracy seems limited by
this smaller number of updates rather than by noise. This, too, is a hypothesis that a run with more meta-steps could
test.

\emph{IFCA.} The loss reports that choose a cluster cost some budget (they raised $\sigma$ by about 6\%, the largest
increase of any mechanism), and clustering did not collapse, but each cluster model is trained by only a third of the clients on average. It therefore
learns more slowly, has the lowest gain and the largest seed-to-seed spread among the personalised mechanisms
($\pm$5.3 pts on the worst-10\%), and costs twice the communication.

\emph{Why q-FFL stalls under DP.} q-FedAvg divides the aggregated update by
$\sum_k\big(LF_k^{q}+qF_k^{q-1}\lVert\Delta w_k\rVert^2\big)$, with $L=1/\eta$. Gaussian noise added to every step
inflates the update norm $\lVert\Delta w_k\rVert$, so the denominator grows and the server step shrinks towards zero.
This matches the flat validation curves at $\eps_0$. Without DP the norm term is smaller but still present, which is
consistent with q-FFL learning more slowly than FedAvg there too. The fairness reweighting never gets the chance to act: instead of raising the
worst clients, q-FFL leaves 85\% of client--seed pairs below DP-FedAvg. A fairness objective whose step size depends
on update norms is thus poorly matched to DP-SGD unless $L$ is retuned for the noise level, which we did not do.

\subsection{Limitations}
The study has clear limits. It uses one image dataset with a synthetic label partition and one small tabular
dataset with a natural partition; FEMNIST and other naturally federated datasets were left out for compute reasons.
Twenty clients with full participation is a cross-silo setting; the conclusions need not carry over to cross-device
FL with thousands of intermittent clients or to client-level DP. The model is deliberately small to fit a 4~GB GPU,
and three seeds limit the statistical power for small differences. The loss-threshold attack is weak, so the
measured leakage is a lower bound; a likelihood-ratio attack~\cite{carlini2022membership} would be stronger. Adult
is dated and narrow~\cite{ding2021retiring}. Finally, the federation is simulated in one process; the Flower
deployment demonstrates the same code over a real network stack but was not used for the measurements.

The results add four limits of their own. All mechanisms share one hyperparameter setting, not tuned per mechanism or
per budget; q-FFL in particular used the standard $L=1/\eta$, and a retuned $L$ might avoid the stall. Per-FedAvg
collapsed to a single predicted class on Adult at $\eps_0$, so its group-fairness gaps there carry no information.
The smallest budget, $\eps=0.3$, was run only for DP-FedAvg and fine-tuning. And because local-only training has the highest
mean accuracy at every private budget where it ran, our ranking speaks to which personalisation mechanism to choose,
not to whether federation pays off under record-level DP at this scale.
